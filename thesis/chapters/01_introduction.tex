\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

\section{Overview}

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated the capability to solve
complex problems~\cite{brown2020fewshot, chowdhery2022palm}, including
mathematical tasks, which makes them valuable
for intelligent tutoring systems, educational technology, and
decision-support tools. As the application of these models expands into
domains requiring reliable multi-step reasoning, it is increasingly
important to evaluate both their final performance and the reasoning
processes employed to reach their conclusions.

This dissertation investigates the mathematical reasoning abilities and
error patterns of three Large Language Models: Gemini Flash Lite
(\texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite}) accessed via Google, GPT-OSS-120B
(\texttt{openai/gpt-oss-120b}) accessed via Groq, and Mistral Large
(\texttt{mistral-large-latest}, which corresponded to Mistral Large 3,
\texttt{mistral-large-2512}, during this project) accessed via Mistral.
Each model was evaluated using two reasoning-based prompting strategies,
Chain-of-Thought (CoT)~\cite{wei2022chain} and Tree-of-Thought
(ToT)~\cite{yao2023tree}, on a common set of 131 mathematics problems
spanning Algebra, Number Theory, Probability, and Combinatorics. The Easy
and Medium problems were sourced from the Hendrycks MATH
dataset~\cite{hendrycks2021measuring}, corresponding to Hendrycks Levels
1--2 and 3--4, while the Hard problems were drawn from the American
Invitational Mathematics Examination (AIME)~\cite{maa_aime}. Administering
all 131 questions with both strategies for each model yielded 786
responses. In addition to evaluating answer correctness, the study
analyses the types of reasoning errors exhibited by each model, the
relationship between step count and answer correctness, and the
computational time required for each strategy.

This chapter introduces the research by presenting the motivation for the
study, defining the research problem, and outlining the research questions
and objectives. It concludes with a summary of the subsequent chapters,
which detail the methodology, experimental procedures, statistical
analyses, and discussion provided in the remainder of the dissertation.

\section{Motivation}

As Large Language Models are used more often in areas that require
reliable multi-step reasoning, such as intelligent tutoring systems and
automated decision-support tools, the way they reach an answer matters as
much as whether the answer is correct. Solving mathematical problems is a
demanding test of this reasoning. Unlike other language tasks, mathematics
requires each step to be logically sound, since a mistake at any point can
invalidate the whole solution. Because of this, it is important to examine
how LLMs reason, not just whether they arrive at the right answer, in
order to judge whether they are reliable and suitable for real-world use.

Reasoning-based prompting methods are now commonly used to help LLMs solve
mathematical problems more effectively. Chain-of-Thought (CoT)
prompting~\cite{wei2022chain} encourages models to show their reasoning
steps, while Tree-of-Thought (ToT) prompting~\cite{yao2023tree} allows
them to explore different approaches before selecting the most suitable
solution. As these techniques become more popular, it is important to
examine not only whether they improve accuracy, but also how they affect
reasoning quality, execution time, and overall mathematical performance.
This highlights the need to systematically evaluate different LLMs on a
range of mathematical problems using these structured prompting
strategies.

\section{Problem Statement}

Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown strong results on mathematical
reasoning benchmarks~\cite{hendrycks2021measuring}, and most studies
measure their abilities by looking at overall answer accuracy. While
accuracy is important, it only gives a broad view of how well a model
works and does not reveal much about the reasoning process behind each
solution. Because of this, current evaluations often do not explain what
kinds of reasoning failures happen, when errors occur, or how different
prompting strategies affect mathematical reasoning.

Reasoning-based prompting methods such as Chain-of-Thought
(CoT)~\cite{wei2022chain} and Tree-of-Thought (ToT)~\cite{yao2023tree} are
now widely used to improve mathematical reasoning. However, simply
comparing their raw accuracy percentages is not enough. Since both
strategies are usually tested on the same questions for a given model, the
results are paired, not independent. This means that any difference in
accuracy needs to be tested for statistical significance. Also, looking
only at final-answer accuracy does not show what types of reasoning errors
the model makes or when those errors first appear.

To address these issues, this dissertation presents a systematic study of
mathematical reasoning in Large Language Models using a curated set of
mathematical problems from different domains and difficulty levels. Three
LLMs are tested with both Chain-of-Thought and Tree-of-Thought prompting.
The study measures answer correctness, types and locations of reasoning
errors, execution time, and overall model performance. It also uses
statistical analysis to compare how different models and prompting
strategies behave. By looking beyond just accuracy, this research aims to
give a fuller picture of the reasoning strengths and weaknesses of today's
Large Language Models.

\section{Research Questions}

This dissertation examines how well Large Language Models handle
mathematical reasoning by exploring the following research questions.

\textbf{RQ1:} What are the most common types of mathematical reasoning
errors made by Large Language Models, and does the distribution of error
types differ across models?

This research question examines whether different Large Language Models
exhibit distinct patterns of mathematical reasoning errors. Since both the
evaluated model and the error type are categorical variables, the study
uses the Chi-square test of independence to determine whether the
recorded distribution of error categories differs significantly across
models. Where expected cell frequencies are small, the results are
interpreted with caution because the Chi-square approximation becomes less
reliable. The assumptions and limitations of the Chi-square test,
including the existence of small expected cell counts in this study, are
discussed further in the Methodology chapter.

\textbf{RQ2:} Is there a descriptive relationship between step count and
answer correctness, and does this pattern differ across models?

This question explores whether longer or shorter reasoning chains tend to
accompany correct or incorrect answers. The analysis compares the mean
and median number of solution steps between correct and incorrect
responses for each model and difficulty tier. This comparison is
descriptive: no formal significance test is applied to the step-count
data, so any observed differences are reported as patterns rather than
statistically confirmed effects.

\textbf{RQ3:} Does Tree-of-Thought prompting significantly improve
mathematical reasoning performance versus Chain-of-Thought prompting for
each Large Language Model?

This question examines whether Tree-of-Thought (ToT) prompting leads to
different answer correctness compared to Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting.
Each mathematical problem is solved using both strategies for the same
model, so the observations are paired. Because of this, McNemar's Exact
Test is used to compare the two prompting strategies, taking the paired
design into account.

Together, these research questions look at different aspects of
mathematical reasoning in Large Language Models. RQ1 studies the types of
reasoning errors made by different models. RQ2 examines the descriptive
relationship between step count and answer correctness. RQ3 compares the
effectiveness of two prompting strategies. Collectively, these questions
provide a thorough assessment of mathematical reasoning that goes beyond
just measuring accuracy.

\section{Thesis Outline}

The rest of this dissertation is structured as follows.

\textbf{Chapter 2 -- Literature Review} reviews the current literature on
Large Language Models (LLMs) and mathematical reasoning. It covers
Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and Tree-of-Thought (ToT) prompting strategies,
reference datasets such as Hendrycks MATH and the American Invitational
Mathematics Examination (AIME), and recent methods for automated
evaluation using LLM-as-a-Judge approaches.

\textbf{Chapter 3 -- Methodology} explains the research
methods used in this study. It describes how the dataset was built, how
models were chosen, how Tree-of-Thought was implemented, the answer
evaluation process, the error classification system, and the statistical
methods used to answer each research question.

\textbf{Chapter 4 -- Application and Case Study} describes how the
experimental pipeline was used to evaluate the final dataset. It reviews
the evaluation dataset in detail, explains how 786 model responses were
collected across different models and prompting strategies, and outlines
the steps taken to ensure the reliability and consistency of the results.

\textbf{Chapter 5 -- Results} presents the experimental results and
statistical analyses. It reports findings for each research question,
compares how models perform with Chain-of-Thought and Tree-of-Thought
prompting, and analyses execution time, answer correctness, reasoning
errors, and other evaluation measures.

\textbf{Chapter 6 -- Discussion} discusses the study's findings in
relation to the research questions and existing literature. It covers
threats to validity, compares the results with prior work, summarises
the findings, and offers suggestions for future research.

\textbf{Chapter 7 -- Conclusion} closes the dissertation by summarising
the overall results and their implications.
