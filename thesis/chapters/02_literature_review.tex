\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{chap:literature}

This chapter reviews the literature underpinning the design of this
dissertation. It examines the two prompting strategies under study,
Chain-of-Thought and Tree-of-Thought, including the gap between the
tree-search design described in the original Tree-of-Thought literature
and the reduced-scale version implemented in this project. It then reviews
the two benchmark sources from which the evaluation dataset is drawn, the
Hendrycks MATH dataset and the American Invitational Mathematics
Examination, before examining LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation methodology and
the self-evaluation bias risk it introduces for this specific study.
Finally, it identifies the literature gap this dissertation's error-type
analysis is intended to address.

The capabilities explored in this dissertation build on earlier research
showing that increasing model size and training data leads to better
general-purpose language skills. Brown et al.~\cite{brown2020fewshot}
showed with GPT-3 that a large enough language model can handle new
tasks using just a few in-context examples, without needing
task-specific fine-tuning. This ability is the basis for the zero-shot
and few-shot prompting strategies examined in this chapter. Chowdhery et
al.~\cite{chowdhery2022palm} continued this trend with PaLM, a
540-billion-parameter model, and found that reasoning and arithmetic
skills kept improving at this scale instead of levelling off. This
result supports the idea that reasoning-focused prompting strategies
like CoT and ToT are worth testing on the largest models available.

\section{Prompting Strategies for Reasoning}
\subsection{Chain-of-Thought Prompting}

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, introduced by Wei et
al.~\cite{wei2022chain}, demonstrated that providing exemplars of
intermediate reasoning steps substantially improves the performance of
sufficiently large language models on arithmetic, commonsense, and
symbolic problem-solving tasks. Rather than mapping a question directly to
an answer, CoT externalises the intermediate reasoning into a generated
sequence of natural-language steps, which the model then conditions on
when producing the final answer. Kojima et
al.~\cite{kojima2022zeroshot} subsequently showed that even a simple
trigger phrase such as ``Let's think step by step'' could elicit similar
reasoning absent hand-written exemplars, indicating that step-by-step
reasoning behaviour is latent within sufficiently large models rather than
something that must be taught anew for each task. Li et
al.~\cite{li2024serial} provide a theoretical account of why this helps:
CoT increases the effective serial computational capacity of transformer
models, which are structurally limited in performing sequential operations
within a single forward pass, by allowing intermediate computational state
to be externalised as generated tokens rather than held only in the
model's internal activations.

This conceptual account also clarifies CoT's limitations. It describes an
increase in computational capacity, not a guarantee of reliability: CoT
makes deeper reasoning possible without guaranteeing that the generated
reasoning steps are logically valid, and because generation is a single
autoregressive sequence, an error introduced early in the chain has no
mechanism for correction or backtracking later in the same response. Wang
et al.~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency} addressed the single-trajectory
aspect of this problem with Self-Consistency, which samples multiple
independent CoT reasoning paths for the same question and selects the
final answer by majority vote. This improves robustness against random
sampling noise in any single trajectory, but the paths it aggregates are
generated independently, with no interaction or evaluation between them
during generation -- if multiple sampled paths share the same underlying
reasoning error, majority voting reinforces the incorrect answer rather
than correcting it. This distinction between independently sampled paths
and an interactively searched space of reasoning steps directly motivates
the alternative approach examined next.

A related line of work improves CoT through training rather than
inference-time sampling: Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024preference} propose
Chain of Preference Optimization, which fine-tunes a model directly on
preferences between correct and incorrect reasoning paths. This
dissertation instead evaluates prompting strategies without additional
fine-tuning, keeping all three models in their off-the-shelf, publicly
served form.

\subsection{Tree-of-Thought Prompting}
\label{sec:tot-scope}

Tree-of-Thought (ToT), introduced by Yao et al.~\cite{yao2023tree},
extended by Long~\cite{long2023tot}, and given a probabilistic
formulation by Cao et al.~\cite{cao2023probabilistic}, reformulates
inference as a search
problem over a structured space of intermediate reasoning states, rather
than a single linear sequence or a set of independently sampled sequences.
In the design described by Yao et al., the model generates multiple
candidate continuations at each step of the reasoning process, evaluates
them against heuristic or model-based criteria, and selectively expands
the most promising branches while pruning others -- a genuine
multi-step search, potentially with backtracking, across a tree that can
grow substantially as reasoning depth increases.

Cao et al.~\cite{cao2023probabilistic} approach branch selection as a
probabilistic scoring problem over possible reasoning paths, instead of
using a single deterministic evaluation, for knowledge-intensive
question answering. While their method is different, it shares the same
goal as the selection step in this dissertation's ToT implementation:
picking the most promising candidate from several independently
generated options.

This distinguishes ToT from Self-Consistency in that branches are
generated and evaluated conditionally rather than sampled independently
and aggregated only at the end, making it better suited to problems that
benefit from active exploration of other reasoning directions.

\textbf{Scope of the Tree-of-Thought implementation in this dissertation.}
The full tree-search design described above -- with evaluation and
selective expansion or pruning repeated at every reasoning step -- is
computationally expensive, since the number of model calls can grow
substantially with reasoning depth and branching factor. The
implementation evaluated in this dissertation is a reduced-scale version
of this design, not the full multi-level search: for each question, three
candidate solutions are generated independently in full (one branching
step, not a per-step tree), after which a single separate selection call
evaluates the three completed candidates and selects the most sound one.
This is stated explicitly here as a scope decision rather than left
implicit, since it means the results in this dissertation characterise a
single-round, breadth-limited form of Tree-of-Thought reasoning rather
than the deeper, iteratively pruned search Yao et al. describe; the cost
and performance consequences of this scope decision are discussed further
in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.

An alternative line of work sidesteps pure prompting strategies
altogether, combining LLMs with external symbolic
solvers~\cite{heyueya2023symbolic} or equipping them with the ability to
call external tools~\cite{schick2023toolformer}. This dissertation
instead evaluates CoT and ToT as prompting-only strategies, without
external tool or solver integration, to isolate the effect of reasoning
structure alone.

\section{Mathematical Reasoning Benchmarks}
\subsection{The MATH Dataset}

The Hendrycks MATH dataset~\cite{hendrycks2021measuring} is a collection
of competition mathematics problems from several subject areas and five
levels of difficulty. Each problem comes with a full step-by-step
solution. This dataset is widely used to test the mathematical reasoning
of language models because it requires multi-step solutions, not just
single-step answers. This makes it useful for studying both the final
answer and the reasoning process. In this dissertation, the Easy and
Medium difficulty levels of the evaluation dataset are taken from
Hendrycks MATH, with problems sampled from the four mathematical
categories used in this study: Algebra, Number Theory, Probability, and
Combinatorics.

\subsection{AIME as a Benchmark Source}

The American Invitational Mathematics Examination (AIME)~\cite{maa_aime}
is a challenging mathematics competition where problems require several
steps to reach a specific integer answer. For this dissertation, the Hard
tier of the evaluation dataset uses only AIME questions, instead of the
hardest problems from Hendrycks MATH. This choice is based on earlier
results from this project, which showed a large accuracy gap between AIME
and Hendrycks questions that were supposed to be equally difficult. This
suggests that the hardest Hendrycks MATH problems are not directly
comparable to AIME's. Using only AIME for the Hard tier keeps the
difficulty level consistent. Like other public mathematics benchmarks,
AIME questions may pose a data-contamination risk because their problems
and solutions are publicly available, a concern documented directly for
a comparable benchmark by Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024gsm1k}. Some of
these or similar problems
might have appeared in the pretraining data of the models tested. This
risk is not unique to AIME and remains a limitation in this study, which
is discussed further in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.

\section{LLM-as-Judge Evaluation}

To automatically determine whether a model's reasoning is correct, and
identify any errors, an evaluator must be able to assess free-text
mathematical reasoning, not just the final answer. Using a separate LLM as
the evaluator, known as LLM-as-a-Judge following Zheng et
al.~\cite{zheng2023judging}, is now common because it can review
open-ended, free-text responses at a scale and cost that human experts
cannot easily match.

This approach has a structural risk that is important for this study. Hao
et al.~\cite{hao2024autorace} point out that when an LLM evaluates
reasoning, including its own or that of similar models, it may be prone to
the same errors as the models it judges. For example, it might miss
logically invalid steps or overlook inconsistencies, just as the original
model might. This is called self-evaluation bias. In this dissertation,
this is especially relevant because Gemini is both the main error-judging
model and one of the models being evaluated, so sometimes Gemini judges
its own reasoning. To address this, the evaluation process includes an
independent validation step. In this step, a fixed sample of responses
judged by Gemini is re-evaluated by a neutral model that is not being
tested, and agreement between judges is measured using Cohen's kappa.
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} explains the purpose, scope, and completion
details of this validation step.

\section{Error Analysis of LLM Mathematical Reasoning}

In addition to the general research on reasoning ability, a separate
body of work examines benchmark robustness specifically. Mirzadeh et
al.~\cite{mirzadeh2025symbolic} found that LLM accuracy on grade-school
arithmetic benchmarks drops when only the numbers in a question are
changed, and drops even more -- by up to 65 percentage points for some
models -- when a plausible but irrelevant clause is added. Li et
al.~\cite{li2024gsmplus} expanded on this with GSM-PLUS. This benchmark
systematically changes grade-school math questions in several ways to
see if accuracy holds up when the phrasing differs from what the model
saw during training. Shi et al.~\cite{shi2023distracted} found a similar
result: LLMs become less accurate when irrelevant context is added,
even if the correct answer stays the same. Zhang et
al.~\cite{zhang2024gsm1k} took a different approach by creating a novel
benchmark that avoids questions likely memorised from the original
GSM8K dataset, and they also saw a drop in accuracy compared to the
original. These results suggest that benchmark accuracy in this area
may overstate true reasoning ability, which is also a concern noted in
this dissertation's data-contamination limitation
(Section~\ref{sec:threats-validity}).

A smaller but growing group of studies focuses on error analysis in LLM
mathematical reasoning~\cite{wang2025survey}. Sun et
al.~\cite{sun2025errorclassification} introduce a flexible framework for
diagnosing errors made by LLMs when solving mathematical word problems,
identifying both the type of error and the step where it happens. Zhang
and Graf~\cite{zhang2025mathematical} also study mathematical computation
and reasoning errors in LLMs, examining what kinds of errors occur and
how they spread across solution steps. Shrestha et
al.~\cite{shrestha2025logical} similarly assess logical and arithmetic
errors across a wide range of numerical inputs, though without a
comparative multi-model design. These studies show that error type
and error location are clear, measurable aspects of LLM mathematical
reasoning failures, and they provide more detailed insights than just
looking at overall answer accuracy.

This dissertation's RQ1 builds on this general approach of classifying
error types, applying it in a comparative way that, to the best of the
author's knowledge, has not been combined in a single study before. The
same closed error taxonomy is used across three different LLMs, tested
with two prompting strategies (CoT and ToT) on the same dataset, and a
Chi-square test of independence is used to check whether the differences
in error type across models are likely real or due to chance. RQ2 extends
this by examining, descriptively, whether the length of a model's
reasoning process relates to whether its final answer is correct.

\section{Summary}

The literature reviewed in this chapter highlights four main points that
this dissertation builds on. First, Chain-of-Thought and Tree-of-Thought
are two distinct ways to prompt multi-step reasoning from LLMs: one uses a
single, externalised sequence, while the other searches through possible
continuations. In this work, the scope of the Tree-of-Thought method is
clearly defined, rather than assumed to match the full tree-search
approach described in earlier studies. Second, Hendrycks MATH and AIME
are both well-known sources of mathematical problems with varying
difficulty, but they are not interchangeable. The decision to use AIME
for the Hard tier in this dissertation is based on data, not just
preference. Third, using an LLM to judge reasoning generated by another
LLM is necessary for a project of this size, but it introduces a risk of
self-evaluation bias. This dissertation aims to measure that risk,
especially since the main judge model is also being evaluated. Fourth,
classifying and locating errors in LLM mathematical reasoning are
important research areas, as shown by Sun et
al.~\cite{sun2025errorclassification} and Zhang and
Graf~\cite{zhang2025mathematical}.

However, to the best of the author's knowledge, error-type analysis has
not previously been combined within a comparative design spanning
multiple models and multiple prompting strategies, supported by formal
statistical testing. This dissertation addresses that gap by comparing
CoT and ToT across three models using the same dataset, classifying
incorrect answers with a closed error-type taxonomy, and testing for
significant differences in error type across models with a Chi-square
test. This approach goes beyond just measuring overall accuracy or
analysing errors in one setting, aiming for a clearer, more detailed
understanding of how these models make mistakes in mathematical
reasoning.
