\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter presents the experimental findings of this study. It
reports overall accuracy, then addresses each research question in turn
(RQ1 error types, RQ2 step count versus correctness, RQ3 CoT versus ToT),
before closing with per-question correctness, judge classification
coverage, and execution time.

\subsection*{Precision of Accuracy Estimates}

The accuracy percentages reported above are point estimates.
Table~\ref{tab:ci-hard} reports their 95\% Wilson score confidence
intervals (Section~\ref{sec:models}) for the Hard tier, where sample
size is smallest ($n=51$) and accuracy estimates are least precise.

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering
  \caption{Hard-tier accuracy with 95\% Wilson confidence intervals.}
  \label{tab:ci-hard}
  \begin{tabular}{llrrr}
    \toprule
    Model & Prompt & Accuracy & 95\% CI & Width \\
    \midrule
    Gemini & CoT & 51.0\% & [37.7\%, 64.1\%] & 26.5pp \\
    Gemini & ToT & 56.9\% & [43.3\%, 69.5\%] & 26.2pp \\
    GPT-OSS-120B & CoT & 56.9\% & [43.3\%, 69.5\%] & 26.2pp \\
    GPT-OSS-120B & ToT & 72.5\% & [59.1\%, 82.9\%] & 23.8pp \\
    Mistral Large & CoT & 47.1\% & [34.1\%, 60.5\%] & 26.4pp \\
    Mistral Large & ToT & 62.7\% & [49.0\%, 74.7\%] & 25.7pp \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Every Hard-tier interval spans roughly 24--27 percentage points,
reflecting the substantial sampling uncertainty at this sample size: for
example, Gemini's 51.0\% CoT accuracy carries a 95\% CI of [37.7\%,
64.1\%], meaning the data are consistent with Gemini's true Hard-tier
CoT accuracy being anywhere from just above one-third to just below
two-thirds. Easy and Medium tier intervals are considerably narrower
(8.8--15.1 percentage points) despite similar sample sizes ($n=40$),
because accuracy in those tiers sits close to the ceiling of 100\%,
where the Wilson interval is naturally tighter. This asymmetry is a
direct consequence of the near-ceiling effect discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:threats-validity}: the Hard tier is simultaneously
where this dissertation's most informative comparisons take place and
where those comparisons are least statistically precise.

Combinatorics is the weakest category for all models. Gemini has its
lowest accuracy here at 72.2\%, Mistral Large at 70.8\%, and

breakdown combines all difficulty tiers, and the Hard tier has uneven

meant to be descriptive. No significance test was done for
category-level accuracy in this dissertation.

\section{Error Type Distribution}
\label{sec:rq1}

Every incorrect response is classified against a fixed taxonomy of 11
error types (Section~\ref{sec:judging}). Before presenting the
distribution of these error types across models, the meaning of each
category is stated explicitly below, together with one real example from
this project's judged data.

\begin{description}
  \item[Arithmetic Error] A plain calculation slip. Example: ``Incorrect
  calculation of 162 times two-thirds.''
  \item[Algebraic Manipulation Error] A mistake while simplifying,
  expanding, or solving an equation. Example: ``Incorrect expansion of
  complex multiplication yielding wrong real part.''
  \item[Probability Reasoning Error] Misapplied independence, conditional
  probability, or normalisation. Example: ``Calculated conditional
  probability incorrectly using flawed enumeration.''
  \item[Combinatorial Counting Error] Over- or under-counting, or the
  wrong choice between permutation and combination. Example: ``Ignored
  leap year occurring in year 0 or negative.''
  \item[Number Theory Error] Wrong handling of divisibility, modular
  arithmetic, or integer properties. Example: ``Failed to correctly
  solve modular congruences for digit replacement.''
  \item[Formula Misapplication] A real formula used, but the wrong one
  for the situation. Example: ``Misapplied Euler's formula for planar
  graphs with boundary arcs.''
  \item[Incorrect Assumption] Reasoning relied on something not actually
  given or true in the problem. Example: ``Assumed starting after leap
  year instead of before.''
  \item[Logical Reasoning Error] A flaw in the deduction itself, not tied
  to a specific mathematical domain. Example: ``Assumed single
  intersection without analyzing periodic components.''
  \item[Incomplete Reasoning] Reasoning stopped short of justifying the
  answer. Example: ``Failed to find the full family of solutions on the
  line segment connecting endpoints.''
  \item[Answer Extraction Error] The reasoning was fine, but no final
  answer could be parsed out of it. In this dataset, its only occurrence
  ($n=1$) was a pipeline fallback rather than a judged example: the
  automatic parser found no final answer at all, so this row was never
  seen by the judge, and only carries the system note ``Parser failed to
  extract the final answer.''
  \item[Other] A catch-all for anything that does not fit the categories
  above. Both occurrences of this label in this dataset ($n=2$) were
  actually judge classification failures after all retries were
  exhausted (system note: ``Classification failed.''), not genuine
  examples of an ambiguous reasoning error -- this is stated plainly
  here rather than presented as if a real example existed.
\end{description}

Figures~\ref{fig:rq1-cot} and~\ref{fig:rq1-tot} show the full Error Type
$\times$ Difficulty breakdown for each model, separately for CoT and
ToT, within the balanced 40 Easy + 40 Medium + 40 Hard question pool (see
Chapter 3).

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{error_type_distribution_by_model_cot.png}
  \caption{Error Type $\times$ Difficulty, by model, CoT (balanced 40/40/40 pool).}
  \label{fig:rq1-cot}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{error_type_distribution_by_model_tot.png}
  \caption{Error Type $\times$ Difficulty, by model, ToT (balanced 40/40/40 pool).}
  \label{fig:rq1-tot}
\end{figure}

Errors are almost entirely concentrated in the Hard tier for every model
and prompting strategy. Easy-tier errors occur only once or twice per
model, and Medium-tier errors occur only once across the entire dataset
(Gemini, ToT). This confirms, at the level of individual errors rather
than aggregate accuracy, that the Easy and Medium tiers carry almost no
diagnostic information about error behaviour; the entire error-type
picture is effectively a Hard-tier picture.

A Chi-square test of independence on the full, unrestricted Model x
Error Type contingency table (all 136 errors across the complete
131-question dataset, not the balanced pool shown above) gives $\chi^2 =
27.95$, $df = 20$, $p = 0.111$. At the conventional threshold of $p <
0.05$, this does not provide statistically significant evidence that the
overall distribution of error types differs across models. This result
carries a caution flag: the minimum expected cell count in this table is
0.265, well below the conventional threshold of 5 below which the
Chi-square approximation becomes unreliable. This non-significant result
should therefore be read as inconclusive given the available sample
size, consistent with the granular breakdown above showing that most
error-type cells contain very few observations per model.

\section{Step Count and Correctness}
\label{sec:step-count}

This section presents the mean and median number of reasoning steps
taken for each model, organised by difficulty tier and whether the final
answer was correct, pooling CoT and ToT responses together for each
model/difficulty/correctness combination. On the Easy and Medium tiers,
all three models perform nearly perfectly: GPT-OSS-120B answers every
Easy and Medium question correctly with both prompting strategies (80 out
of 80 in each tier), and Gemini and Mistral Large each have only one to
four incorrect responses in these tiers combined. With so few incorrect
cases, it is not possible to make a meaningful comparison between correct
and incorrect step counts except on the Hard tier, where all three models
have a substantial number of both correct and incorrect answers (36 to 47
incorrect responses per model).

On the Hard tier, Gemini and GPT-OSS-120B show almost no difference in
step count between correct and incorrect responses: Gemini's mean step
count is 7.85 for correct answers and 7.77 for incorrect ones, and
GPT-OSS-120B's is 5.95 for correct and 6.14 for incorrect. For these two
models, a longer or shorter reasoning process does not seem to be linked
to whether the final answer was correct.

Mistral Large shows a clearly different pattern. Its incorrect Hard-tier
answers have a mean step count of 16.17 (median 12.5), compared with a
mean of 13.68 (median 8.0) for correct answers. The median is
substantially higher for incorrect answers (56\% higher than for correct
answers), while the mean is also higher but by a smaller margin (18\%
higher). Figures~\ref{fig:step-count-cot} and~\ref{fig:step-count-tot}
show the step count per question for CoT and ToT and illustrate this
visually. Mistral Large's markers are consistently more spread out and
reach further into the 20 to 50 step range than Gemini's or
GPT-OSS-120B's, especially on the Hard tier. In contrast, GPT-OSS-120B's
markers form the tightest and most consistent cluster of the three models
across all difficulty tiers.

\section{CoT vs.\ ToT}

This section examines whether Tree-of-Thought (ToT) prompting leads to
significantly different answer accuracy compared to Chain-of-Thought
(CoT) prompting for each model. The analysis uses McNemar's Exact Test on
paired results from all 131 questions.

When combining all three difficulty tiers, every model shows higher
overall accuracy with ToT than with CoT: Gemini goes from 79.4\% to
80.9\%, GPT-OSS-120B from 83.2\% to 89.3\%, and Mistral Large from 78.6\%
to 84.7\%. However, because each model answers the same 131 questions
with both strategies, these percentages cannot be directly compared. Only
the questions where the two strategies give different answers (the
discordant pairs) show which strategy performs better.

For Gemini, there are 10 discordant pairs: 4 questions are correct only
with CoT, and 6 are correct only with ToT. McNemar's exact test gives
$p = 0.754$, which is not significant. Although ToT shows higher raw
accuracy, this difference could easily be due to chance because there
are so few discordant pairs.

For GPT-OSS-120B, there are also 10 discordant pairs, but the results are
much more one-sided: only 1 question is correct only with CoT, while 9
are correct only with ToT. This gives $p = 0.021$, which is significant
at the 0.05 level.

For Mistral Large, the result is the most one-sided: out of 8 discordant
pairs, none are correct only with CoT, and all 8 are correct only with
ToT. Every disagreement between the two strategies favours ToT. This
gives $p = 0.008$, which is the strongest evidence among the three
models that ToT affects performance.

Overall, these results do not support a general claim that ``ToT
improves performance.'' The improvement is statistically significant for
two of the three models (GPT-OSS-120B and Mistral Large), but not for
Gemini, where the higher ToT accuracy could be due to chance because
there are so few discordant pairs. The per-tier accuracy results in
Table~\ref{tab:accuracy-difficulty} show that this pattern is most
noticeable in the Hard tier. In the Easy and Medium tiers, accuracy is
already very high for all models, so there is little room for a
prompting strategy to make a measurable difference.

\section{Question-Level Correctness Across Conditions}

This section examines correctness by focusing on each question instead
of each model. It considers how consistently each question is answered
across all six model-and-prompt combinations, which include three models
and two prompting strategies.

For the Easy and Medium tiers, correctness is nearly the same across all
conditions. Out of 80 questions, 77 (96.25\%) are answered correctly by
all six model and prompt combinations, and none are answered incorrectly
by all six. Only three questions show any differences between
conditions. This means these tiers are solved for all three models, no
matter which prompting strategy is used.

The Hard tier is quite different. Out of 51 Hard-tier questions, 10
(19.6\%) are answered incorrectly by all six conditions, while 13
(25.5\%) are answered correctly by all six. The other 28 questions
(54.9\%) have mixed results, with at least one condition getting the
answer right and at least one getting it wrong. Figure~\ref{fig:correctness-heatmap-hard}
shows this in detail. Each row represents a Hard-tier question with at
least one incorrect answer, and each column shows one of the six model
and prompt combinations, coloured by whether the answer was correct.

\section{Judge Classification Coverage}

Out of 786 total responses, 136 were marked as incorrect and needed to be
sorted into one of the 11 error-type categories described in
Section~\ref{sec:judging}. Every incorrect response was given a specific
error-type classification, so none were left unclassified. This complete
coverage was ensured by the \texttt{assert\_no\_stale\_error\_labels}
check described in Section~\ref{sec:data-quality}, which requires that
each incorrect response has an error-type label. As a result, no
response could move through the process without being classified.

Of those 136 incorrect responses, two categories served as fallback
labels instead of real error diagnoses: Answer Extraction Error (1
response) and Other (2 responses), as explained in
Section~\ref{sec:rq1}. These two categories covered 3 out of 136
incorrect responses (2.2\%). For the other 133 responses (97.8\%), the
judge used one of the nine main error-type categories instead of a
fallback label.

A separate validation step was planned in which a neutral judge model,
not itself one of the three models under evaluation, would independently
classify a sample of the same responses, with agreement measured using
Cohen's kappa. This would have checked for self-evaluation bias arising
from Gemini judging its own responses alongside those of the other two
models. This validation was attempted but not completed, as the neutral
judge's provider imposed a daily token limit that the study's scale
exceeded (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). This gap is reported here
rather than omitted, since Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} discusses what
it does and does not undermine about the error-type findings.

\section{Execution Time}
\label{sec:execution-time}

Execution time is reported here for transparency, not as a formal
research question. By construction, ToT costs approximately four times
the generation calls of CoT per question (three branch generations plus
one selection call, versus a single CoT call), so a substantial increase
in wall-clock time under ToT is expected.

Pooling across all three difficulty tiers, mean execution time per
response is 12.64s (CoT) versus 31.62s (ToT) for Gemini, a 2.50x
increase; 9.77s versus 44.55s for GPT-OSS-120B, a 4.56x increase, closest
to the theoretical 4x call-count ratio; and 43.42s versus 130.80s for
Mistral Large, a 3.01x increase. Mistral Large is markedly slower than
the other two models under both prompting strategies, consistent with
its free-tier rate limit of 2 requests per minute described in
Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}.

Figure~\ref{fig:execution-time-tot} shows this per question under ToT:
Mistral Large's Hard-tier execution times are the most extreme, with
several questions exceeding the 300s axis cap (labelled individually up
to 894s). The corresponding CoT figure, where execution times are
comparatively unremarkable, is not shown here.
